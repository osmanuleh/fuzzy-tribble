01 — The Network

Britain's gas transmission backbone

National Gas Transmission owns and operates the National Transmission System — the sole high-pressure gas network in Great Britain. Gas enters at coastal terminals such as St Fergus, Bacton, Easington and Teesside, and is pushed across the country entirely by compression.

The NTS operates at up to 85 bar — roughly 1,200 times atmospheric pressure. It is the only gas system in Great Britain that uses compression; lower-pressure regional distribution networks rely on the pressure difference that compression creates at the handover point.

02 — How It Works

What a compressor station actually does

Gas entering the NTS at coastal terminals is at high pressure. As it travels along the pipe, friction losses reduce that pressure continuously. Without intervention, the gas would slow and eventually stop. Compressor stations are placed at intervals to restore the pressure.

Each station contains two or more compressor units — each one an industrial jet engine (gas turbine) driving a centrifugal compressor. The turbine burns a small portion of the flowing gas to generate the shaft power needed to drive the compressor.

STEP 01

Gas arrives at low pressure

After 80–160 km of travel, pipeline gas may have lost 20–30 bar. It enters the station via inlet isolation valves and a scrubber to remove any liquids or particulates.

STEP 02

Gas turbine fires up

A small proportion of the pipeline gas fuels the gas turbine driver — aeroderivative turbines adapted from jet engines, capable of starting in minutes and reaching 10–20 MW.

STEP 03

Centrifugal compressor raises pressure

The turbine drives a centrifugal compressor at high speed. The impeller adds kinetic energy to the gas; a diffuser converts that energy into pressure. Typical ratio: 1.2–1.5:1.

STEP 04

After-cooling and reinjection

Compression heats the gas significantly. Air-cooled heat exchangers bring it back down before it re-enters the pipe — denser cool gas means more mass throughput.

03 — The Physics

Why compression heats the gas — and why that matters

When a centrifugal compressor squeezes the gas, it does work on it. By the first law of thermodynamics, in an adiabatic process all that work raises the gas temperature.

T₂ / T₁ = (P₂ / P₁) ^ ((γ − 1) / γ)
Isentropic compression · γ ≈ 1.3 for natural gas · T in Kelvin · P in any consistent unit

For a pressure ratio of 1.4:1 with gas entering at 15 °C, the outlet temperature rises to approximately 52 °C — a 37 °C rise. After-coolers are essential: hot gas is less dense, reducing the throughput benefit of compression.

W = Cp × T₁ × [(P₂/P₁)^((γ−1)/γ) − 1] / η
Shaft work per kg of gas · Cp ≈ 2,220 J/kg·K · η = isentropic efficiency (78–85%)

A typical NTS compressor handling 150 kg/s at a 1.4:1 pressure ratio requires roughly 8–12 MW of shaft power — similar to a small offshore platform's total electrical generation.

ΔP_friction ∝ (v² × L × ρ) / D
Darcy–Weisbach · v = velocity · L = pipe length · ρ = gas density · D = pipe diameter

This explains why compressor stations exist: velocity squared and pipe length drive losses up. Building a wider pipe is often more expensive than adding a compressor station.

04 — The Turbines

Industrial jet engines driving the NTS

National Gas describes its compressor units as "jet engines" — and this is literally accurate. The existing fleet includes the Rolls-Royce Industrial Avon, derived from the Avon jet engine that powered aircraft like the English Electric Lightning and Hawker Hunter. These cores were adapted for industrial gas compression by coupling them to a power turbine and centrifugal compressor.

In April 2025, National Gas announced over £350 million for new equipment at Peterborough, St Fergus and Wormington, manufactured by Siemens Energy, Baker Hughes and Solar Turbines.

Existing fleet

Rolls-Royce Industrial Avon

Aeroderivative of the Avon jet engine. The Avon 200 series produces ~15–16 MW. The NTS fleet has collectively logged tens of millions of operating hours on this platform.

New (2025+)

Siemens Energy turbines

Part of the £350m upgrade at three priority sites. Meet tighter NOₓ limits under the Medium Combustion Plant Directive (MCPD) and are hydrogen-blend ready from commissioning.

New (2025+)

Baker Hughes & Solar Turbines

Also part of the 2025 programme. Modern aeroderivative and industrial compressor trains, hydrogen-blend capable, supporting ~1,000 jobs across Great Britain.

The MCPD requires that any impacted asset must be made compliant, removed from service, or limited to 500 hours per year by 1 January 2030. Wormington is one of five priority MCPD sites agreed with Ofgem for near-term investment.

05 — The Future

From natural gas to hydrogen

National Gas has begun the FutureGrid Compression project — testing whether the NTS compression system can be repurposed to carry hydrogen rather than (or blended with) natural gas. Hydrogen is the leading candidate for decarbonising industrial heat that cannot easily be electrified.

FutureGrid Compression programme

A test compressor system has been relocated to the Spadeadam facility with a 1 km pipe loop, for trials with hydrogen blends up to 20%. The gas turbine driver is being tested separately at the Siemens Energy/RWG facility in Ansty. New compressors installed in 2025 are already specified to compress hydrogen blends from day one.
